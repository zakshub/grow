# Career Discovery Engine

## Purpose

The Career Discovery Engine is the core intelligence module of Grow.

Its job is not to ask a participant which career they want and then decorate that answer.

Its job is to help a participant who does not know their direction discover plausible paths through structured evidence.

## Core question

What kind of work is this person likely to find meaningful, sustainable, learnable, and economically realistic given their traits, interests, strengths, values, constraints, and observed behaviour.

## Inputs

The engine combines evidence from eight sources.

1. Personality tendencies
2. Interest patterns
3. Strength and aptitude signals
4. Work values
5. Preferred work environment
6. Education and life constraints
7. Practical task performance
8. Career market evidence

## Discovery flow

### Stage 1: Context

Collect only enough background to interpret later answers.

Fields may include:

1. Age
2. Education level
3. Current study or work status
4. City and region
5. Access to phone, computer, and internet
6. Family responsibilities
7. Financial constraints
8. Languages used comfortably

### Stage 2: Open discovery

Ask story based questions before formal scoring.

Examples:

1. What kind of task do people usually ask you to help with
2. Which school or life problems do you enjoy solving
3. What do you learn on your own without being told
4. When have you felt proud of something you made or solved
5. Which activities make you lose track of time
6. What type of work quickly becomes boring
7. If you had one free month to learn something, what would you try

The purpose is to reveal patterns before the participant sees categories.

### Stage 3: Structured trait assessment

Measure dimensions such as:

1. Logical reasoning
2. Verbal expression
3. Creativity
4. Numerical reasoning
5. Spatial reasoning
6. Social awareness
7. Persuasion
8. Leadership
9. Detail orientation
10. Organisation
11. Research curiosity
12. Practical orientation
13. Independence
14. Collaboration
15. Risk tolerance
16. Ambiguity tolerance
17. Persistence
18. Learning agility
19. Stress tolerance
20. Digital fluency

### Stage 4: Interest discovery

Explore broad interest clusters without relying only on direct self report.

Potential clusters:

1. Practical and hands on
2. Analytical and investigative
3. Creative and expressive
4. Social and helping
5. Enterprising and persuasive
6. Organised and process oriented

Interest evidence can come from:

1. Explicit preferences
2. Repeated topic choices
3. Voluntary activities
4. Past projects
5. Media and learning behaviour
6. Practical trials
7. Follow up behaviour

### Stage 5: Work values

Measure what the participant wants from work.

Examples:

1. High income
2. Stability
3. Independence
4. Social impact
5. Recognition
6. Creativity
7. Flexible schedule
8. Remote work
9. Predictable routine
10. Leadership
11. Intellectual challenge
12. Job security
13. Fast entry into earning
14. International mobility

### Stage 6: Constraints

Career fit without feasibility is incomplete.

Capture constraints such as:

1. Education affordability
2. Time available for training
3. Family restrictions
4. Mobility
5. Device access
6. Internet quality
7. Academic prerequisites
8. Need to earn quickly
9. Geographic limitations
10. Care responsibilities

Constraints should influence route planning, not become assumptions about ability.

### Stage 7: Career cluster generation

Generate several plausible career clusters rather than one answer.

Each cluster should be supported by evidence.

Example clusters:

1. Software and engineering
2. Data and analytics
3. Design and creative production
4. Teaching and learning
5. Business and sales
6. Operations and administration
7. Research and science
8. Healthcare
9. Law and policy
10. Skilled trades
11. Media and communication
12. Finance and accounting
13. Community and social services
14. Entrepreneurship

The exact taxonomy should evolve with research and Pakistan market needs.

### Stage 8: Practical experiments

Assign short, low cost tasks representing the work rather than only the subject.

Examples:

Software candidate:
Solve a small logic and build task.

Teaching candidate:
Explain a concept to a beginner and reflect on the experience.

Design candidate:
Improve a simple interface or communication problem.

Sales candidate:
Write and deliver a short persuasive pitch.

Research candidate:
Investigate a question, compare sources, and summarise findings.

Operations candidate:
Plan and organise a small multi step task.

### Stage 9: Evidence update

After each experiment, capture:

1. Completion
2. Quality
3. Persistence
4. Enjoyment
5. Curiosity after completion
6. Need for external pushing
7. Learning speed
8. Desire to repeat

This evidence can raise or lower recommendation confidence.

### Stage 10: Ranked recommendation

Return a ranked set of directions.

For each direction show:

1. Fit level
2. Supporting evidence
3. Conflicting evidence
4. Required education or training
5. Time to entry
6. Cost considerations
7. Pakistan opportunity
8. Remote opportunity
9. Earning range when reliable data exists
10. Risk factors
11. Recommended next experiment
12. Confidence

## Example reasoning

A participant may score high in logic, curiosity, detail orientation, and independent work preference.

This does not automatically produce programmer.

The engine might generate:

1. Software development
2. Data analysis
3. Cybersecurity
4. Engineering
5. Research
6. Financial analysis

Then practical tasks and contextual constraints separate the options.

Another participant may show strong verbal expression, social awareness, persuasion, and energy from interaction.

Possible clusters might include:

1. Teaching
2. Sales
3. Business development
4. Law
5. Media
6. Human resources
7. Public relations

Again, the recommendation remains multi option until stronger evidence exists.

## Recommendation confidence

Suggested levels:

### Low confidence

Evidence is incomplete or contradictory.

### Moderate confidence

Several signals align but practical evidence is limited.

### High confidence

Personality, interests, strengths, values, constraints, and practical behaviour show repeated alignment.

High confidence should still not be described as certainty.

## Career reality layer

Recommendations should eventually be enriched with current evidence about:

1. Required qualifications
2. Training pathways
3. Entry level roles
4. Local hiring demand
5. Remote work relevance
6. Freelance relevance
7. Typical starting pay
8. Growth potential
9. Automation exposure
10. Geographic concentration

Market evidence must include source date and confidence because labour markets change.

## Output structure

A participant report should contain:

1. Personal pattern summary
2. Strongest observed traits
3. Strongest interest signals
4. Demonstrated strengths
5. Important work values
6. Key constraints
7. Top career clusters
8. Career directions inside each cluster
9. Why each direction fits
10. Main tradeoffs
11. Practical experiments completed
12. Recommended next action
13. Areas still uncertain

## Failure modes to avoid

1. Mapping one trait directly to one career
2. Treating personality type as destiny
3. Overweighting stated interests
4. Ignoring family and financial reality
5. Recommending only fashionable technology careers
6. Confusing English fluency with intelligence
7. Giving precise salary promises without evidence
8. Ignoring practical task behaviour
9. Producing too many career options without ranking
10. Hiding uncertainty

## Long term learning

The engine should improve when Grow learns which recommendations led to:

1. Continued exploration
2. Course completion
3. Degree choice satisfaction
4. Skill acquisition
5. Employment
6. Sustained interest
7. Career switching success
8. Participant regret
9. Human reviewer correction

The goal is not to become more confident over time.

The goal is to become more accurate and better calibrated.
